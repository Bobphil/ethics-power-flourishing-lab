The Challenge

Across twelve chapters, you built a Tool Rack.

You learned to ask about attention, reasons, formation, contingency, rules, authority, motivation, respect, consequences, background power, fairness, care, and effective capability. The point was never to discover one Tool that replaces all the others.

Real systems rarely present only one ethical problem at a time.

A college policy can help some students and burden others. A prediction can be useful without being certain. An institution can have a legitimate purpose while using a questionable rule. A support program can increase welfare while treating a student as an object to be managed. A formally equal service can be much easier for some students to use than for others.

This Lab receives the Tool Record you built across the historical chapters and asks you to do something different from the historical chapters.

You will not learn a thirteenth theory.

You will decide which of the Tools you already earned actually belong in one complicated problem, what order to use them in, how strongly your evidence supports a judgment, what repair would target the problem you found, and what would make you reconsider.

The governing movement is:

MAP → TRIAGE → SELECT → SEQUENCE → JUDGE → REPAIR → REOPEN

A strong response does not use all twelve Tools. In most cases, three to five primary Tools will be enough.

The question is not:

Which ethical theory wins?

The question is:

What does this system require us to notice, which Tools help us see it, what can we responsibly conclude, and what should happen next?

Two Organizing Questions: Power and Flourishing

Power in this Lab means the capacity to shape the conditions under which other people act, choose, are represented, contest decisions, or reopen what has been decided.

Power is not automatically illegitimate. A college needs power to register students, award grades, distribute aid, enforce safety rules, and organize programs. The ethical question is what power is doing, who can exercise it, what limits it, and whether people subject to it can challenge or repair its use.

Flourishing asks what forms of agency, learning, security, relationship, development, and real opportunity a system is supposed to make more possible.

Flourishing is not one required life plan. A system may support one capability while weakening another. A policy intended to help students persist may, for example, improve access to advising while reducing privacy or practical control over registration.

Power and Flourishing are not new Tool Cards. They are the two organizing questions that help you decide which existing Tools belong in the case.

The Case: Pathway Support System

A system built to help

Harbor County College is a fictional open-access public college with about 18,500 students spread across several campuses and online programs.

College leaders are worried about students disappearing from courses without asking for help. Advisors say that they often learn about a student's difficulty only after several missed assignments, a failed course, or a withdrawal. Faculty members report that they cannot always tell whether a quiet student is doing well, struggling privately, working long hours, caring for family members, or preparing to leave college.

The college therefore pilots the Pathway Support System, or PSS.

PSS is intended to identify students who may need support before a problem becomes a crisis.

Every week, the system creates a support-priority score from 0 to 100.

It uses information already held by the college:

  • course activity recorded in the learning-management system;
  • submitted and missed assignments;
  • grades already entered by instructors;
  • credits attempted and earned;
  • course withdrawals and repeated courses;
  • registration changes;
  • financial-aid or payment holds;
  • tutoring and advising contacts; and
  • selected categories from advisor records, such as “requested academic support” or “difficulty contacting student.”

The model does not directly use race, sex, disability diagnosis, medical records, religion, citizenship, or disciplinary history as inputs.

Scores from 70 to 100 are placed in a high-priority advisor queue. Scores from 50 to 69 are marked moderate priority. Scores below 50 produce no special flag.

The score is recalculated weekly.

The pilot rule says the score is supposed to be used for support, not punishment.

That sounds simple.

The practice is less simple.

What happens after a flag

A high-priority student receives an email and text message saying that the college would like to connect the student with support.

The assigned advisor also receives an alert.

If the student responds, the advisor can discuss tutoring, emergency grants, course planning, financial-aid questions, transportation, food support, counseling referrals, technology access, or other services.

If the student does not respond after seven days and a second outreach attempt, the advisor may place a temporary advising check-in hold on future registration changes. The hold does not remove the student from current classes, but the student must communicate with an advisor before changing or adding courses for the next term.

The college describes this as a way to make sure that students who appear to be struggling do not disappear without contact.

Some students describe it differently.

What students can see

Students receive a general privacy notice stating that academic and administrative information may be used to improve student support.

They are not automatically shown:

  • their PSS score;
  • the exact variables contributing to the score;
  • how much weight each variable receives; or
  • why the threshold is set at 70.

A student may ask an advisor why they were contacted. Advisors can see the broad categories contributing to the flag, but they do not see the full model or feature weights.

If a college record is factually wrong, a student can use existing procedures to correct the record.

There is no separate process for appealing the PSS score itself.

The score is not placed on the academic transcript.

PSS records are retained for two years for evaluation of the pilot.

What the pilot appears to show

PSS was tested for two semesters in 18 high-enrollment gateway courses.

About 2,400 students were included.

During the pilot:

  • 720 students received at least one high-priority flag;
  • advisors made successful contact with 520 of those students;
  • 41% of the contacted students accepted at least one support service;
  • 61% of contacted high-priority students enrolled the following term;
  • 49% of high-priority students whom advisors did not successfully reach enrolled the following term;
  • 76% of students who never received a high-priority flag enrolled the following term;
  • 84 students received an advising check-in hold;
  • 17 of those students later reported that the hold delayed a desired registration change; and
  • 22% of high-priority flags were later annotated by advisors as requiring important context that the model did not contain.

The institutional-research team gives a careful warning.

The pilot was not randomized.

Students successfully reached by advisors may differ from students who could not be reached. Students who were never flagged differ from flagged students in many ways. The numbers therefore do not prove that PSS caused the higher continuation rate among contacted students.

The team concludes:

The pilot provides evidence that targeted outreach may be useful, but it does not yet establish the size of the system's causal effect on persistence.

The college has also not yet completed a full subgroup-impact audit. Protected characteristics are not direct model inputs, but the research team notes that apparently neutral variables can still interact with unequal background conditions.

Six views from inside the system

1. Tasha — the outreach helped, but the score did not understand her

Tasha is 31 and completing prerequisites for a health program. She works evenings and helps care for her mother.

She downloads readings when she has reliable internet and often studies offline. Her learning-management-system activity therefore looks irregular. During one difficult month she misses two assignment deadlines but later completes both.

PSS flags her as high priority.

An advisor contacts her and discovers that a transportation problem and an unexpected utility bill have put her close to dropping a course. The advisor helps her apply for a small emergency grant and connects her with a revised course plan.

Tasha stays enrolled.

She says the advisor helped.

She also says:

“If the college had simply asked whether I needed help, that would be one thing. What bothered me was learning later that a system had decided I was a risk before I even knew I was being scored.”

She does not think the program should be eliminated. She wants students to know more about how the representation is produced and to have a way to challenge it.

2. Malik — the system saw a problem that was partly in the college's own data

Malik is 19 and taking four courses.

One instructor records grades in batches rather than weekly. For nearly three weeks, several completed assignments appear in the system as ungraded. During the same period Malik misses one advising email because he has changed phone numbers.

PSS flags him as high priority.

He is surprised because he believes he is doing well.

A temporary advising check-in hold appears shortly before registration opens. Malik contacts the advising office, but the earliest appointment is four days later. By the time the hold is removed, the section of a required course that fit his work schedule is full.

The college later confirms that Malik had completed the assignments.

No individual intended to block his schedule.

The hold still changed his options.

3. Jonah — the flag caught a problem before he asked for help

Jonah is 22 and works variable hours.

His first exam score is low. He then misses two assignments and begins opening course materials less often.

He tells no instructor that his work hours have increased.

PSS flags him.

An advisor reaches him, helps him compare course options, and connects him with tutoring. Jonah later says that he probably would not have contacted anyone on his own.

He passes the course.

He describes the system as intrusive but useful.

4. Reina — the advisor who must turn a score into a human conversation

Reina advises more than 300 students.

Before PSS, she says her outreach often depended on whichever student happened to contact her first, a faculty referral, or a problem already visible in a record.

PSS gives her a way to prioritize limited time.

She also worries about what the score does to her own attention.

“When a student appears in red at the top of a queue, I have to remind myself that red means the system wants me to look. It does not mean I already know what is wrong.”

Reina sometimes adds context to a student's record after a conversation, but she cannot directly change the model.

She supports continuing PSS but opposes using the score for discipline, scholarships, admissions, or program eligibility.

5. Professor Chen — help can become expectation

Professor Chen teaches one of the gateway courses.

During the pilot, instructors do not see the numerical score. They sometimes receive an “outreach recommended” notice.

Chen appreciates earlier advising contact. She also notices a possible danger.

If faculty begin to expect flagged students to fail, the flag could shape the very environment in which those students are trying to succeed.

She asks:

“Are we identifying a need, or are we quietly creating a category of student?”

6. The Student Success Council — a decision has to be made

The college's Student Success Council must recommend what happens next.

The president wants a decision before the next registration cycle.

The council is considering whether to:

A. Expand PSS college-wide in its current form.

B. Expand it, but modify the design before expansion.

C. Continue a limited pilot while repairing specific features and gathering stronger evidence.

D. Keep voluntary outreach but suspend registration holds and other consequential uses until stronger justification exists.

E. Pause or discontinue PSS.

The council is not required to choose one of these exact options. It may recommend a bounded alternative.

Its decision must answer two questions:

What should the college do now?

and:

How strongly does the present map justify that action?

Phase 1 — MAP

Do not begin by choosing your favorite theory.

First reconstruct the system.

Your map should identify:

People and positions. Who is represented in the case? Who is missing or weakly represented?

Decision points. Where does a person or system make a consequential choice?

Data flows. What enters the PSS representation? What does not?

Benefits. What does the system make easier, safer, or more possible?

Burdens. What costs, risks, restrictions, labels, or hidden work does it create?

Power. Who can alter another person's options, representation, access, or ability to contest?

Unknowns. What would you need to know before making a stronger judgment?

Then sort your map into three categories:

ESTABLISHED IN THE CASE — information the dossier actually gives you.

INFERRED — conclusions you are drawing from that information.

UNKNOWN / MISSING — information that would matter but is not available.

First Firewall

A score about a student ≠ the student.

Second Firewall

A useful prediction ≠ a complete explanation of why an individual student is struggling.

Phase 2 — TRIAGE

Now identify the ethical layers actually present.

You are not yet selecting philosophers.

Ask which kinds of problems appear in the system.

For example, you might find problems involving incomplete representation, weak evidence for a causal claim, rules and exceptions, institutional authority, perspective and stereotyping, respect for agency, benefit and harm, coercion, background inequality, fairness of institutional design, care burdens, or real access to support.

Do not assume every layer is equally important.

Your job is to identify the three to five most decision-relevant layers.

A useful test is:

If this layer were removed from the case, would my recommendation likely change?

If not, it may be secondary rather than primary.

Phase 3 — SELECT

Choose three to five primary Tools from the frozen Tool Rack.

For each selected Tool, state four things:

Fit. What feature of this case makes the Tool relevant?

Question. What does the Tool ask here?

Contribution. What does it help you see that another selected Tool might miss?

Limit. What can the Tool not establish by itself?

A weak selection says:

“I chose Kant because autonomy is important.”

A stronger selection says:

“I chose the Respect / Duty / Autonomy Audit because the college acts on a representation students do not automatically see or control. The Tool helps test whether support preserves the student as an agent rather than merely managing the student as a risk object. It does not by itself tell us whether the program improves persistence.”

That last sentence matters.

Tool selection includes Tool limits.

Phase 4 — SEQUENCE

Put your selected Tools in an order.

There is no single required sequence.

But your order must make intellectual sense.

Suppose you choose:

Ethical Attention → Background Power → Consequence/Welfare/Harm → Respect/Duty/Autonomy → Dependency/Effective Capability

You might explain the order this way:

  1. Ethical Attention first separates what the system actually observes from the interpretation “student at risk.”
  2. Background Power then examines how the college's categories, incentives, schedules, and data system shape the choice before any single advisor acts.
  3. Consequence/Welfare/Harm asks what the system actually does to different people.
  4. Respect/Duty/Autonomy tests whether beneficial outreach is being achieved in a way that preserves the student's standing as an agent.
  5. Dependency/Effective Capability asks whether the support actually becomes usable opportunity under real conditions.

Another student might reasonably begin with Reason-Giving because the college is making a causal claim about effectiveness, then move to Legitimacy, Consequences, Fairness, and Capability.

Both sequences could be strong.

The important question is:

Why does the next Tool need the map produced by the previous one?

Sequencing is part of the reasoning.

Phase 5 — JUDGE

Now make a bounded judgment.

Choose the status that best fits your present map:

INSUFFICIENT MAP

The available evidence does not justify the consequential decision currently being proposed.

This does not always mean “do nothing.” A low-risk temporary action may still be warranted while information is gathered.

PROVISIONALLY ACTIONABLE

The map is incomplete, but it contains enough of the right things to justify a limited, revisable action.

The action should not be given more standing than the evidence has earned.

STRONGER JUDGMENT WARRANTED

The evidence and reasoning are strong enough to support a more confident institutional decision.

Even here, REOPEN remains possible.

Then state your recommendation.

Examples of recommendation forms include:

Continue voluntary outreach, but suspend registration holds until the college establishes a clearer authorization, contest process, and evidence of benefit.

or:

Continue the pilot with specified transparency and appeal repairs, while delaying college-wide expansion until subgroup effects and causal evidence are better understood.

or:

Expand the support function because the benefits are sufficiently supported, but prohibit the score from being used in program eligibility, discipline, scholarships, or other higher-stakes decisions.

These are examples of forms, not model answers.

You may reach a different conclusion.

Your recommendation must include:

What should happen now?

What should not happen yet?

How strongly is your conclusion warranted?

What does your evidence still not show?

Phase 6 — REPAIR

A repair is not the sentence:

“Make the system more ethical.”

A repair targets an identified failure mode.

For each repair, specify:

Failure mode. What exactly is going wrong?

Repair. What should change?

Responsible actor. Who has the power and standing to make the change?

Expected improvement. What problem should the repair reduce?

Possible new burden. What could the repair itself make worse?

Evidence. How would we know whether the repair worked?

Possible repair targets might include student notice and explanation; score visibility; a way to contest or correct the representation; separation of supportive from coercive uses; registration-hold rules; advisor caseload; faculty access to flags; model-input governance; subgroup-impact auditing; retention of records; human review; documentation of exceptions; or limits on future uses.

Do not assume that more transparency, more human review, or more data is automatically better. Each repair can create new costs.

Phase 7 — REOPEN

A responsible decision includes a route back into inquiry.

Your REOPEN plan must identify at least three triggers.

A trigger should be concrete enough that another person could recognize when it occurred.

Weak:

“REOPEN if something changes.”

Stronger:

“REOPEN if students receiving registration holds show a higher rate of lost course access than otherwise similar students who receive outreach without holds.”

Weak:

“REOPEN if students do not like the system.”

Stronger:

“REOPEN if recurring student reports show that PSS explanations are too vague to let students identify or contest the basis of a high-priority flag.”

Your REOPEN plan should answer:

What result or evidence would make you reconsider?

Who may raise the challenge?

Who must review it?

What part of the decision becomes open again?

REOPEN does not mean the original decision was necessarily wrong.

It means the system was not granted permanent moral authority merely because it passed one review.

Quick Tool-Selection Check

Before submitting, ask:

  • Did I use three to five primary Tools rather than trying to impress the reader by naming all twelve?
  • Can I explain why each selected Tool fits this case?
  • Can I say what each Tool does not establish?
  • Does the order of my Tools matter?
  • Did I distinguish a useful prediction from a complete representation of a person?
  • Did I distinguish benefit from authorization?
  • Did I identify who has power to change the system?
  • Did I identify what people are actually able to do and be, not only what resources are offered?
  • Did I make a decision rather than hiding behind “more information is needed”?
  • If I said more information is needed, did I specify what information and what decision it would affect?
  • Did I propose a repair that targets a failure mode?
  • Could someone tell when my REOPEN condition had been triggered?

What a Strong Lab Demonstrates

A strong Lab does not prove that the student has memorized twelve philosophers.

It demonstrates that the student can enter a complicated situation, build a bounded map, choose the Tools that fit, resist conclusions the Tools do not justify, make a decision strong enough for the evidence, target a repair, and preserve a route for correction.

That is the mature form of the course's recurring question:

Is the present map sufficiently warranted for the decision this situation actually requires?

The Lab ends where responsible ethical action begins:

Act from the best sufficiently warranted map the task currently permits, but do not give the judgment more standing than the map has earned.
